                                       Lecture 8A: Visual Imitation
                                                        Abstract
           Despite its popularity, behavior cloning cannot serve as an analogue for how children learn sensorimotor
       skills from adults. According to Smith and Gasser (2005), social learning is how we acquire skills. Therefore,
       this lecture focuses on visual imitation, a robot learning technique that uses high-fidelity computer vision
       algorithms to extract information from humans, such as reference motions and subroutines in monocular
       videos, to teach robots to navigate in and interact with the world.


14.1      Introduction
Visual imitation learning enables robots to acquire complex skills by observing human demonstrations.
Compared to the reinforcement learning that requires dedicated reward design and the behavior cloning that
requires laborious robot data collection, visual imitation learning opens up a new path that leverages the
human demonstrations from daily video while enabling robot skill learning.
Visual imitation mirrors how human babies learn. Human children begin to understand the world through
observation and perception, with their closest role models often being their parents. By watching these
parent demonstrations, babies learn fundamental skills like grasping, pushing, and walking. These skills
remain adaptable despite changes in the children’s bodies as they grow (e.g., in limb length, joint angles, and
strength). Smith and Gasser (2005) outline six lessons we can learn from human babies: (1) be multi-modal,
(2) be incremental, (3) be physical, (4) go to explore, (5) be social, and (6) use language. Specifically, visual
imitation learning focuses on the fifth lesson, teaching robots to “socially” learn from human demonstrations
using visual perception in a way similar to human babies. In the following sections, we will discuss how we
leverage computer vision techniques to perform visual imitation with robots.

14.2     SFV: Reinforcement Learning of Skills from Videos
Peng et al. (2018b) propose a method to enable physically simulated characters to learn skills from videos
(SFV). This is a data-driven approach to animation that uses deep pose estimation and deep reinforcement
learning on publicly available YouTube videos. The goal of this work is to query videos demonstrating desired
behavior that a character controller can imitate. The character controllers are robust to environmental
changes, perturbations, and retargeting to various morphologies. In prior work, Peng et al. (2018a) introduced
DeepMimic, a data-driven RL-based motion imitation technique that utilizes reference motion data. The
main novelty of SFV is that it combines components proposed in prior works. Specifically, it uses vision-based
pose estimators Kanazawa et al. (2018); Wei et al. (2016) to produce reference motions that an RL-based
motion imitation algorithm learns to reproduce with an animated character. Thus, rather than acquiring
reference motions from motion capture, it is acquired from monocular video with Wei et al. (2016) and
Kanazawa et al. (2018), 2D and 3D pose estimators respectively, which are consolidated to get a reference
trajectory. Overall, SFV shows that vision algorithms designed to recover 3D information from monocular
video allow for high-fidelity motion imitation for dynamic skills, such as cartwheels, flips, spins, and dances.
See Figure 52 for a qualitative example of SFV results for backflips.

14.3     Reconstructing 4D Human Actions
Human videos provide crucial demonstrations for robot learning, but extracting 4D actions remains challenging.
From a single frame, the vision model maps a 2D observation (x, y) to a 3D state (x, y, z). In video frames,
this evolves to a 3D observation (x, y, t) mapping to a 4D representation (x, y, z, t) to capture dynamic actions
over time. The concept is shown in Fig. 53. In the following sections, we illustrate how this fundamental

14.4     Humans in 4D
To achieve the goal of reconstructing a 4D (x, y, z, t) state from videos is not easy because of the unknown
camera poses and the non-rigid human shapes. A top-down solution is that we can leverage the known human
prototype to reduce the solution spaces of the human states. This idea is used in Kanazawa et al. (2018),
which proposes to first leverage the human-generating SMPL model Loper et al. (2023) to generate a human
prototype embedding and then regress the inverse parameters like the camera poses, human poses, and the
shapes. In detail, SMPL is a differentiable model that generates a 3D mesh by shaping a template body
based on specific parameters, adjusting joint positions through forward kinematics, and deforming the surface


                                                       86
with linear blend skinning. Then Kanazawa et al. (2018) uses 3D keypoints for measuring reprojection error
and applies linear regression to get the final mesh vertices. Afterwards, it leverages an iterative regression
strategy to get the camera poses, human poses, and the shapes, which mimics the traditional optimization
paradigm to estimate the inverse-graphic parameters. A GAN is also employed to verify the reconstructed
4D human, ensuring that the appearance is logical. Building on this work, Goel et al. (2023) advances 4D
human reconstruction via better representation models such as VIT which inherit the human manifold and a
larger dataset which benefits the model learning more general human representations. Both these two works
highlight the importance of operating on the manifold of human shapes; otherwise, 3D distortion can occur if
only 2D correspondence points are ensured. For example, in Fig. 54, the wrong reconstruction results can be
solved if we inject the human manifold prototype into the models.

14.5    PHALP: Tracking People by Predicting 3D Appearance, Location & Pose
Rajasegaran et al. (2022) propose Predicting Human Appearance, Location and Pose (PHALP) for tracking
humans in 3D from monocular video. The two key insights from this work are that occlusion is hard to
disambiguate in 2D, and 3D appearance is invariant to viewpoint and pose. Therefore, tracking is performed
in 3D. Given the 2D bounding box for each person in the monocular frame, the person is lifted to a 3D
representation that includes 3D appearance, pose, and location. Initially, the single frame 3D features are
estimated with an HMAR model Rajasegaran et al. (2021). Then, a temporal model is formed to make
tracklet predictions, where 3D appearance is aggregated using the canonical UV map of the SMPL model,
pose is aggregated with a modified HMMR model Kanazawa et al. (2019), and 3D location is processed with
linear regression. The similarities between the tracklets’ predicted states and the current frame observations
are computed, since there is invariance built into this 3D representation. Finally, association is performed
with the Hungarian matching algorithm. In addition to achieving state-of-the-art results with tracking metrics
like Identity switch (IDs), Multi Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA), ID F1 score (IDF1), and Higher Order
Tracking Accuracy (HOTA), PHALP demonstrates strong qualitative results in scenarios where the humans
are in unusual poses and there is crowding.

14.6    Humanoid Locomotion as Next Token Prediction
Radosavovic et al. (2024) cast real-world humanoid control as a next-token prediction problem using a
causal transformer trained via autoregressive prediction. Specifically, humanoid robot action prediction is
high-dimensional with multiple degrees of freedom. Drawing inspiration from large language models that
take advantage of the self-supervised nature of next-token prediction which enables scaling laws, Radosavovic
et al. (2024) model the joint data distribution of sensorimotor trajectories and transform them into token
representations so that it can be modeled as next-token prediction. The method overview is shown in Fig. 56.
Concisely, Radosavovic et al. (2024) build their own dataset that comprises trajectories from neural network
policies, model-based controllers, motion capture data, and YouTube videos, allowing for leveraging vast and
diverse sources. However, there are many incomplete trajectory data due to the data collection issues from
vision data such as occlusion, early termination, or trajectory failure oscillation. The paradigm of next-token
prediction can still make full use of this incomplete data. This approach enables a full-sized humanoid robot
to perform zero-shot walking in real-world environments like San Francisco after training on only 27 hours of
data and to generalize to new commands such as walking backward. These findings highlight a promising path
toward learning complex real-world control tasks through generative modeling of sensorimotor trajectories.

14.7    Learning Navigation Subroutines by Watching Videos
Inspired by the Vauquois triangle for machine translation Vauquois (1968), Saurabh Gupta presents a control
hierarchy shown in Figure 57. This triangle connects human actions, robot actions, and the observed world.
Essentially, human actions are connected to the observed world through human behavior characterized with
muscle tensions at the lowest level, followed by macro actions, skills, sub-goals, and goals in increasing order
of abstraction. Analogous to human muscle tensions, robot actions are characterized by motor torques. Thus,
using their perception of the world, robots must generate behaviors that are executed as low-level robot
actions using task definitions, dense rewards, and subroutines and affordances.
This idea has been successfully applied to navigation by Kumar et al. (2020). In this paper, the authors learn
from egocentric human videos, which provide the agent’s perceptual input but lack action information. To
compensate for the missing action labels, a self-supervised inverse model is trained on random interaction
data and is run on the aforementioned egocentric videos to obtain pseudo-labeled actions between consecutive
image pairs. Then, two models are learned for downstream navigation tasks. The video trajectories are
divided into smaller sub-trajectories. These sub-trajectories are passed into an encoder that produces a
latent which is predictive of the action to be taken, given every video frame in the sub-trajectory. Then, an
affordance model is trained to predict which subroutines can be invoked for a given input image from the set
of learned subroutines. Additionally, the subroutines and affordance model are fine-tuned with reward-based
RL, which significantly improves sample complexity. Overall, the subroutine and affordance models are used

                                     Lecture 8B: Visual Imitation
                                                     Abstract
           In this lecture, we explore visual imitation in the context of manipulation, which presents greater
       challenges compared to locomotion and navigation. We examine recent advances in hand-object recon-
       struction, including groundbreaking work in reconstructing interactions in unconstrained environments.
       The lecture covers the challenges of transferring learned behaviors from human demonstrations to robotic
       systems, the importance of affordances in manipulation, and the evolution of approaches to functional
       grasping. We conclude by discussing current efforts to learn from demonstrations "in the wild" and the
       remaining challenges in this field.


15.1      Introduction
Visual imitation in the context of manipulation presents significantly greater challenges than locomotion
and navigation tasks. While the reconstruction of whole-body movements has largely been solved, the
accurate reconstruction of hand-object interactions remains an active area of research. Current techniques
predominantly rely on 2D approaches, as direct 3D reconstruction faces substantial challenges due to occlusions
and the complexity of hand-object interactions.

15.2      Hand-Object Reconstruction




           Figure 59: The Hand Object Reconstruction Problem, as presented by Cao et al. (2021)

Recent work by Cao et al. (2021) has demonstrated significant progress in reconstructing hand-object
interactions in unconstrained environments. Their research showed how to simultaneously reconstruct both
hands and objects in 3D from natural interactions, providing a foundation for understanding manipulation
tasks. Building on this work, Pavlakos et al. (2024) presented advances in hand reconstruction using
transformer-based approaches at CVPR, showing marked improvements in accuracy and robustness.
The evaluation of hand-object reconstruction methods requires careful consideration of several factors. Systems
must demonstrate the ability to handle a wide variety of poses, maintain robustness when objects are partially
occluded during manipulation, and achieve accurate reconstruction even with limited visible information.
These criteria are particularly challenging when dealing with complex object interactions in natural settings.
Currently, hand-object reconstruction is estimated to be only about 50% solved. This limitation stems from
several fundamental challenges. During manipulation, often only a small fraction of the object is visible,
making reconstruction difficult. Additionally, there is typically insufficient variation in parallax or disparity in
viewpoints to employ traditional reconstruction methods, such as structure-from-motion approaches. Even if
the object is moving, successful reconstruction often requires substantial top-down knowledge and familiarity


                                                         90
with object categories to estimate object motion, making it particularly challenging for novel or unusual
objects.

15.3     Multiview Compressive Coding




           Figure 60: One solution to hand-object reconstruction, presented by Wu et al. (2024)

Wu et al. (2024) made significant contributions through their work on applying Multiview Compressive
Coding (MCC) for 3D reconstruction. Their approach predicts the occupancy and color of query points to
form the final 3D model. The reconstruction process follows a sequential approach, first constructing the 3D
hand model and then reconstructing the object in the context of the hand. This method cleverly utilizes
prior knowledge of human hand form factors to inform object dimensions and positioning.
The integration of CAD models through Large Language Models (LLMs) represents an innovative approach to
improving reconstruction accuracy. By leveraging existing 3D models and fitting them to observed interactions,
the system can better handle cases where direct reconstruction from visual data alone would be challenging.

15.4     Transfer Learning and Retargeting
The transfer of learned behaviors from human demonstrations to robotic systems presents a complex challenge
in the field of visual imitation. A significant portion of current work focuses on collecting human demonstrations
within laboratory settings, but the field’s ultimate goal is to leverage internet-scale demonstration data. This
approach would allow robots to learn from a much broader range of experiences and scenarios.
When considering the transfer of human demonstrations to robotic systems, several levels of abstraction must
be addressed. For instance, if we can successfully create 3D reconstructions of human-object interactions from
large datasets like Ego-4D (Grauman et al. (2022)) and 100DOH (Shan et al. (2020)), we must then solve
the retargeting problem to apply these interactions to robotic systems. One promising approach involves
the use of reinforcement learning within physics simulators to adapt human hand movements to robotic
configurations.

15.5     Learning from Human Demonstrations
Human demonstrations provide rich sources of information beyond mere physical movements. When observing
a simple action, such as opening a cabinet, multiple levels of information can be extracted. The demonstration
reveals common sense knowledge about typical cabinet contents, affordances of the cabinet door, and specific
subroutines such as grasping patterns. Understanding these various levels of information is crucial for
developing robust robotic manipulation capabilities.

15.6     Affordances and Interaction Analysis
The analysis of human hands during object interaction provides crucial insights for robotic manipulation.
Hands serve to localize and stabilize active objects, indicate potential interaction points in scenes, and reveal
information about object states and appropriate interaction methods. This understanding has led to the
development of the affordances or "hotspot" concept in recent research, which focuses on identifying and
characterizing common interaction points on objects.
The field of robotic grasping has evolved significantly from traditional force closure approaches to more
sophisticated functional grasping strategies. Multiple functional grasps often exist for a single object, and
the appropriate grasp depends heavily on context. For instance, handling a clean mug for serving differs
substantially from handling a dirty mug for washing, both in terms of grasp location and approach strategy.

15.7     Learning from "In-the-Wild" Demonstrations

representations for how the environment needs to move in an embodiment-agnostic manner.

Recent work, exemplified by the Chang et al. (2024), addresses the challenge of learning from unconstrained
demonstrations. This approach tackles the fundamental problem of transferring human demonstrations to
robotic systems by factoring out the human presence from the scene. The system separates the environment
state from human presence through sophisticated segmentation and inpainting techniques, allowing robots to
focus on the environmental transformations rather than trying to replicate human appearances.
The system demonstrates impressive capabilities in generalizing to novel environments and objects beyond
the training set. This ability to work "beyond lab workspaces" represents a crucial step toward developing
robots that can function effectively in diverse real-world situations. However, significant challenges remain in
achieving reliable zero-shot transfer to entirely novel scenarios.
Bahl et al. (2023) leverages human videos to learn an affordance model : that predicts contact heatmaps and
post-grasp trajectory waypoints for objects in the scene. During deployment, the robot grasps the object at
the mode of the heatmap and follows the post-grasp trajectory to execute the task.

15.8     Conclusion
While significant progress has been made in visual imitation for manipulation tasks, several crucial challenges
remain. The field continues to work toward more robust solutions for learning from human demonstrations
"in the wild," with the ultimate goal of enabling robots to learn from unconstrained human demonstrations
on platforms like YouTube. Future work must address the integration of physics-based approaches with
learning methods, improve retargeting solutions, and enhance generalization capabilities beyond laboratory
environments.
