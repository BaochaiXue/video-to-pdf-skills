       Lecture 3A: The Human Hand and Dexterous Object Manipulation

5.1    Human Hand
Human hands have two main types of grip, as shown in Fig. 14, that are crucial for performing a wide range
of tasks: precision and power.

The opposable thumb allows for force application in “opposition” to the fingers. This is defined by the
thumb’s ability to reach the other fingers on the hand and the volume of the space that this closure wraps.
This ability is essential for grasping and manipulating objects, enabling humans to hold and control tools,
write, and perform fine motor tasks with precision. The opposable thumb grants a unique advantage in
achieving secure and stable grasps, particularly during power grips and pinch motions.

The manipulability ellipsoid defines the finger dexterity across different positions. The volume of this
ellipsoid is a metric for dexterity, representing the range of directions in which the fingers can move efficiently.
A larger manipulability ellipsoid indicates a higher degree of dexterity, allowing for more precise and versatile
finger movements. This is crucial for tasks that require fine manipulation, such as threading a needle or
typing. The balance between thumb opposition and finger dexterity is what enables the human hand to
perform both powerful and delicate tasks with remarkable control.




                      Figure 14: The Two Primary Types of Grip: Precision and Power



There is a sensorimotor continuum of the human hand’s function. In order of sensory to motor:

    • Passive tactile sensing

    • Active haptic sensing

    • Prehension

    • Non-prehensile skilled movements

5.2    Robot Hands
5.3    Design Principles
Designing an effective robotic hand requires careful consideration of two fundamental aspects: kinematics
and mechanics.

1. Kinematics – what joint configurations are the best? Joint configuration determines the hand’s
range of motion and task capability. To balance dexterity and control, joint design should mimic human
hand movements while remaining simple. Optimal configurations enable a wide variety of tasks like gripping
and precision handling.

2. Mechanics – how do we build it reliably in hardware? The mechanical design must ensure
durability and efficiency. Choosing reliable materials, addressing tendon routing, motor placement, and load
distribution are crucial for long-term functionality. Practical considerations like maintenance and cost are
also important for real-world usability.

5.4    Joint and Actuation Design
Motors as Joints. In robotic hands, motors often act as the joints themselves, controlling movement. A
key design challenge arises in balancing the use of large, powerful motors, which are often bulky, with smaller,
weaker motors. In contrast, human hands achieve dexterity and strength through a unique arrangement:
most of the power is generated by larger muscles in the arm, not in the hand, allowing the hand itself to
remain light and nimble.

Linkage Joints. The Four-Bar Linkage is a commonly used mechanism that moves power generation out
of the fingers and into other parts of the robotic arm, as shown in Fig. 15. This design reduces the number of
motors directly embedded in the fingers, leading to lower degrees of freedom but often resulting in increased
brittleness and reduced durability.




                                                      30
                             Figure 15: Robotic Hand Built with Linkage Joints


Tendon-Driven Hands. Tendon-driven systems mimic the structure of human tendons, using motors
located in the arm to drive finger movements. These systems are widely used in robotic hands, such as the
RoboNaut Hand, Shadow Hand, DLR Hand, DASH Hand, and LEAP Hand v2. Tendons, typically made
from materials like fishing line or durable synthetic fibers, transmit motor forces to control finger articulation.
The mechanism usually consists of two rigid bodies rotating against each other, providing a single degree of
freedom per joint. While this type of actuation is popular for its biomimetic qualities, it is susceptible to
damage from off-axis forces, which limits its robustness. Tendon-Driven Hands usually contain the following
types of joints:

   1. Revolute Joints. These are simple rotational joints that allow parts of the robotic hand to rotate
      around a fixed axis, typically supporting a single degree of freedom. Revolute joints are a common and
      straightforward solution but often lack the flexibility required for more complex motions.

   2. Rolling Joints. Rolling joints offer more degrees of motion than tendon-driven or revolute systems,
      allowing for greater dexterity. However, they are difficult to manufacture due to their reliance on sliding
      contact between parts, which increases friction and wear over time.

   3. Flexure Joints. Flexure joints use flexible materials, such as thin plastic or rubber, to create bending
      motion without the need for traditional mechanical parts. An example is the "living hinge" found in
      shampoo bottle caps. These joints can be cast or 3D printed, providing a cost-effective, low-friction
      solution, though they are typically limited in terms of strength and durability.

Hydraulic Robot Hands. Hydraulic actuation involves the use of high-pressure fluid to generate significant
force, making hydraulic hands capable of powerful movements. However, these systems are difficult to maintain
due to their complexity and are challenging to manufacture, making them less common in practical robotic
applications.

5.6    Metacarpophalangeal (MCP) Joint Design
The ball-and-socket joint at the human knuckle, known as the Metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint, cannot be
directly replicated in robotic hands with traditional direct-driven systems. Instead, it is typically approximated
using two separate motors. In previous robotic hand designs (as shown in Fig. 17), such as the Allegro
Hand, a loss of degrees of freedom (DOFs) often occurs during power grasps, while designs like the C-Hand
experience a reduction in DOFs during precision grasps. This limitation stems from the absence of an effective
MCP joint design.

Gaps still exist between the LEAP hand and the human hand. The LEAP hand has less strength and
dexterity than a human hand. Additionally, its rigid design means that it is less robust to collisions than soft
hands.

   2. Human hands have skeleton and skin layers, unlike robotic models.

   3. Dexterous kinematic structure in robots needs improvement.

   4. Lack of proper thumb opposability.

   5. Limited grasp types compared to human hands.

                    Lecture 3B: Proprioception and Tactile Perception
                                                    Abstract
          This lecture covers touch, proprioception, and general concepts behind the human hand.


6.1      The Sensorimotor Contunuum
The hand serves the dual purpose of acting as a sensor and an actuation device. Different parts of the hand
fulfill each task, forming a sensorimotor continuum.

6.2      The Hand as a Sensor
For sensing, when objects come into contact with the fingers, they perform passive tactile sensing. Similarly,
the fingers and hand can be moved to perform active haptic sensing, such as weighing the difference in weight
between two objects in our hands. In psychology experiments, humans are asked to hold an object of weight
of W1 and an object of W2 and then guess the difference in weight. Interestingly, humans discern a perceived
change relative to the pre-existing stimulus. This means that if a person holds a block weighting W1 = 106g
and then picks up a block weighting W2 = 100g, they are unlikely to perceive a difference. On the contrary,
if W1 = 16g and W2 = 10g, it is likely the person would perceive the difference.

This is a phenomenon described by Weber’s Law and is formulated as Weber constrast:

                                                    ∆W
                                                       =k
                                                     W
where:

    • ∆W is the smallest detectable difference in weight.

    • W is the original weight.

    • k is the Weber fraction, a constant characteristic of the sensory modality.

This means that if a person holds a block weighing W = 100 g and then picks up a block weighing
W + ∆W = 106 g, they may not perceive a difference because the relative difference is small:

                                             ∆W    6g
                                                =       = 0.06
                                              W   100 g
On the contrary, if W = 16 g and ∆W = 6 g, the relative difference is larger:

                                             ∆W    6g
                                                =      = 0.375
                                             W    16 g
Making it more likely that the person would perceive the difference. Active movement improves the accuracy
of weight discrimination compared to passive holding because it involves additional proprioceptive cues from
muscles and joints.

6.3    The Hand as an Actuator
The hand can also be an action device, applying forces to affect the world. Hand movements are separated
into prehensile and non-prehensile movements. Prehensile movements involve grasping like holding a tool,
pick and place tasks, and gripping a ball. Non-prehensile movements involve manipulating an object without
grasping it like pushing a button playing the piano, or poking Oski the bear.

6.4    Proprioception
Originally in "De Anima" Aristotle described five senses: sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell. In 1826
Scottish scientist and surgeon Bell proposed a sixth sense, the "muscular sense," which was renamed
to "proprioception" by Sherrington. Proprioception refers to perceiving "about yourself," referring to the
awareness of the position or movement of the body or parts of the body relative to one another. Proprioception
is divided into two parts: the musculoskeletal system, pertaining to sensors in the muscles, tendons, and
joints, and the vestibular system, pertaining to the inner ear and your sense of balance. The musculoskeletal
system (kinesthesia) relies on sensors inside the muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs, and joint receptors. In
the vestibular system’s inner ear, the semicircular canals measure rotation, and otoliths measure translation,
similar to an IMU.




                                     Figure 19: Musculoskeletal Sensing




                                                      35
6.5    Analog in Robotics
To mimic the musculoskeletal system, robots use joints driven by electric motors with rotating shafts, which
apply torque in response to control inputs, analogous to muscles generating force. Rotary encoders measure
the angular position of the motor shafts, providing feedback on joint angles and velocities. By integrating
sensors similar to muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs, robots can achieve proprioceptive capabilities,
allowing them to sense their own joint positions, velocities, and applied forces.

6.6    The Sense of Touch
When the skin is contacted by an external stimulus, its surface is indented or stretched due to its elasticity.
The mechanical deformation, which can be a surface area increase of as much as 70%, is detected by
specialized sensory receptors called mechanoreceptors, which signal the location of the contact, the force
exerted, and motion speed. Your subjective perception of skin contact includes sensations such as touch,
pressure, stroking, and vibration, as interpreted by your brain. Different types of mechanoreceptors are
responsible for detecting different types of stimuli. The most sensitive areas are typically "glabrous" skin, or
areas lacking hair.




                                         Figure 20: Touch Receptors



6.7    Mechanoreceptors
There are four main types of mechanoreceptors in the skin of the hand Kandel et al. (2013).

   • Merkel Disk Receptors (SA1): These slowly adapting type I receptors are sensitive to sustained
     pressure and fine spatial details. Located just below the epidermis, they have small receptive fields and
     are densely packed in the fingertips, allowing for high-resolution tactile discrimination.

   • Meissner Corpuscles (RA1): These rapidly adapting type I receptors respond to light touch and
     low-frequency vibrations (up to 50 Hz). Situated superficially in the dermis, they are sensitive to
     changes in texture and detect slip, which is important for grip control.

   • Ruffini Endings (SA2): These slowly adapting type II receptors respond to skin stretch and contribute
     to the perception of object motion and hand shape. They have large receptive fields and are located
     deeper in the dermis.

   • Pacinian Corpuscles (RA2): These rapidly adapting type II receptors detect deep pressure and
     high-frequency vibrations (up to 1000 Hz). They are located deep in the dermis and subcutaneous
     tissue and are sensitive to minute subcutaneous displacements.


                                                      36
                                         Figure 21: Mechanoreceptors


Mechanoreceptors differ in their receptive field sizes and adaptation rates. The receptive field refers to the
area of skin where stimulation affects the firing of a particular neuron. Merkel cells and Meissner corpuscles
have small receptive fields (approximately 1 mm), allowing for precise spatial resolution. Ruffini endings and
Pacinian corpuscles have larger receptive fields, contributing to broader spatial perception.

Adaptation rate describes how quickly a neuron stops firing in response to a constant stimulus. Slowly
adapting (SA) receptors continue to respond to a sustained stimulus, providing information about static
aspects of touch, such as pressure and shape. Rapidly adapting (RA) receptors respond only at the onset and
offset of a stimulus, detecting dynamic changes such as motion and vibration.



6.8    Examples

Finding a coin in your pocket without visual input
As your fingers touch various objects, the Merkel cells help detect edges and fine textures to identify the coin,
among other items like keys or a wallet. Meissner corpuscles detect the coin’s slip as you adjust your grip,
prompting subconscious adjustments in grip force. Subconsciously, your brain rapidly adapts to this sensor
feedback, allowing you to make adjustments like increasing friction through your fingertips.



Braille reading
Braille is a tactile writing system used by people who are visually impaired, consisting of patterns of raised
dots in a 3x2 grid. The high density of Merkel cells in the fingertips allows Braille readers to discriminate
these patterns effectively. Average Braille readers can read 120 words per minute, with experts reaching up
to 200 words per minute. An average visual reader can read 240 words per minute. Braille reading also
demonstrates how sensing is done with both your sensors and your brain.

6.9    Analog in Robotics
We aim to equip our robots with tactile sensing capabilities analogous to human receptors. When a stimulus
is applied to a robot’s finger or hand, the response becomes distributed across the surface, similar to the
activation of Pacinian corpuscles in humans. Although the precise mechanisms are not fully understood,
psychophysical studies indicate that humans can discriminate between two stimuli separated by approximately
one millimeter on the finger. This sensor spacing closely matches the distribution of our robotic tactile sensors



6.10     Somatosensory Cortex
The somatosensory cortex, located adjacent to the central sulcus in the cerebral cortex, is responsible
for processing sensory information from various body regions, including the hands, fingers, tongue, lips, and
ears. Sensory signals from peripheral nerves in the hand travel through the spinal cord to the brain, where
they are received by the somatosensory cortex. This area is closely situated next to the motor cortex,
which governs voluntary movements such as hand and finger motions. The proximity of these two cortices
facilitates the integration of sensory inputs and proprioceptive feedback, enabling coordinated motor actions.




                        Figure 22: Somatosensory Cortex and Coronal Cross-Section

M1 is the primary motor cortex, controlling voluntary movements, and M2 is involved in planning and
coordinating movements, labeled similar to V1 to V8 which are visual areas, with V1 handling basic visual
processing and V4 for color, V5 for motion, etc. S1, S2, and S3 are somatosensory areas, where S1 processes
touch and body sensations, and S2/S3 handle more complex sensory integration. Together, these regions
work to process and respond to sensory inputs, guiding perception and movement.Kandel et al. (2013)

Human studies by Penfield in the 1950s, have mapped the somatosensory cortex, revealing a sensory-motor
homunculus that illustrates the body’s representation within the cortex. Different body parts occupy
varying amounts of cortical real estate based on their sensory and motor importance. For example, the fingers
have a high density of receptors and correspondingly larger cortical areas, analogous to the fovea in the visual
system, which has a high density of photoreceptors.

6.11     Analog in Robotics
The concept of cortical plasticity, where frequent use of certain body parts, such as pianists’ fingers, leads
to expanded cortical areas—suggests that robotic systems can adapt sensor distributions based on usage
patterns to enhance performance and adaptability.

Overall, leveraging the organization and functionality of the human somatosensory and motor cortices enables
the development of sophisticated robotic systems capable of nuanced tactile perception and coordinated

6.12     The Ventral and Dorsal Streams
In the case of vision, you as a human have around a hundred million receptors. A single receptor can vary
from detecting a single photon to detecting a flood of photons in bright sunlight, providing a dynamic range
of 11 orders of magnitude. Visual information enters the brain via the thalamus, which acts as a preliminary
processing hub before relaying signals to the somatosensory cortex. From there, the visual system bifurcates
into two primary streams: the ventral stream and the dorsal stream. The ventral stream, often referred
to as the "what" pathway, extends to the inferior temporal cortex and is responsible for object and face
recognition. In contrast, the dorsal stream, known as the "where" or "how" pathway, projects to areas
associated with the somatosensory and motor cortices. It integrates proprioceptive and tactile information
to guide actions, such as adjusting grip strength when an object begins to slip. Note attributes of objects
are judged visually, but also through our proprioceptive and somatosensory systems. Both systems exist for
vision and in the setting of proprioceptive information and touch information.

6.13     Robotics Analogs
The ventral stream parallels Vision Language Models (VLMs) in artificial intelligence, which excel at identifying
and describing objects by converting visual inputs into language tokens. This is akin to to multi-modal
conscious perception encoding object names and attributes. However, while VLMs predominantly mimic the
ventral pathway, they focus purely on perception without addressing the spatial and action-oriented aspects
of vision akin to the Dorsal pathway. In general there is much more research today in robotics addressing
vision, but there is little progress in proprioception.

6.14     Active Haptic Sensing
Active haptic sensing involves intentional movements of the hand to explore objects, integrating tactile and
proprioceptive information. This contrasts with passive touch, where the hand remains stationary, and stimuli
is applied. These procedures demonstrate how active movement enhances sensory perception by engaging
different mechanoreceptors and proprioceptive feedback. Lederman and Klatzky identified several exploratory
procedures used in haptic perception Lederman and Klatzky (1987):



                                                       39
                                     Figure 24: Cortical Connections


   • Lateral Motion: Moving fingers across a surface to assess texture.

   • Pressure: Applying force to determine hardness or compliance.

   • Static Contact: Placing a hand on an object to sense temperature.

   • Unsupported Holding: Lifting an object to perceive weight.

   • Enclosure: Wrapping the hand around an object to gauge global shape and volume.

   • Contour Following: Tracing the edges of an object to discern its exact shape.

6.15    Prehensile and Non-Prehensile Movements
The hand’s ability to manipulate objects is categorized into prehensile and non-prehensile movements. These
classifications were defined by Napier, highlighting the different functional requirements of various tasks
Napier (1956), and Noriko Kamakura, who proposed a detailed taxonomy of grasps based on contact patterns
and functions Kamakura (1989).

Prehensile movements involve grasping objects and are further divided into the following classifications:

   • Power Grasp: Using the whole hand to grasp an object, maximizing force and stability (e.g., holding
     a hammer). This grip involves wrapping the fingers around the object with the thumb reinforcing the
     grip.

   • Precision Grasp: Using the fingertips to manipulate small objects with precision (e.g., holding a
     pencil or threading a needle). This grip allows for fine control and manipulation.

Non-prehensile movements involve actions where the hand does not grasp an object but interacts with it
through pushing, tapping, or other means. Examples include gesturing, typing, and piano playing, which
require precise timing and coordination.




                                                    40
6.16     Analog in Robotics
Understanding the variety and functionality of grasping and movement patterns is essential for designing
robotic hands. Integrating tactile sensors into robotic hands is crucial for fine manipulation and safe in-
teraction with objects and humans. Advancements in materials science, sensor technology, and machine
learning are contributing to progress in this area. However, current robotic tactile sensing is significantly less
sophisticated than their human counterparts.

Challenges include:

   • Sensor Development: Creating tactile sensors with spatial and temporal resolution comparable to
     human mechanoreceptors.

   • Data Processing: Managing and interpreting large amounts of sensory data in real-time.

   • Control Integration: Incorporating sensory feedback into control algorithms to adjust movements
     adaptively.
