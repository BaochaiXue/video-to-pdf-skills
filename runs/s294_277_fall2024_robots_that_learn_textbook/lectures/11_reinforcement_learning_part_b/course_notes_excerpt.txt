                                Lecture 6B: Reinforcement Learning
                                                       Abstract
           Reinforcement Learning (RL) has influenced in many ways the field of Robotics. These methods
       enabled the achievemnt of complex tasks without needing explicit, physics-based models. Instead, they
       offer methods to approximate optimal control policies through interaction with the environment. In this
       lecture, we focus on the practical aspects of RL. In particular, we discuss the need for fast, high-quality
       simulation environments, and approaches to tackle the ubiquitous Sim2Real gap. We also some examples
       of successful real-world RL applications.


12.1      Introduction
The lecture focused on the challenges of sim-to-real transfer in robotics, listing some advanced simulators and
highlighting how discrepancies between simulated environments and the real world can hinder the performance
of policies trained via reinforcement learning. It discussed key strategies to bridge this gap, including the
use of parallel simulation for high-speed data generation and domain randomization techniques—specifically
dynamics and visual randomization—to enhance model generalization. Real-world successes were presented,
such as OpenAI’s dexterous in-hand manipulation and high-speed quadrotor flight, demonstrating how
these methods enable effective transfer of simulation-trained policies to physical robots. Overall, the lecture
emphasized the importance of accounting for real-world variabilities to improve the robustness and adaptability
of robotic systems in practical applications.

12.2      Simulation environments
Although there are some examples of successful application of RL directly in the real world, such as Haarnoja
et al. (2018), this is often impractical due to safety, cost and time constraints. Simulation is a valuable
resource to overcome these limitations. A lot of work has gone into developing fast, efficient simulators. The
main options are:

   • MuJoCo Todorov et al. (2012) (Multi-Joint dynamics with Contact). This is an open-source physics
     engine from Google DeepMind, developed for efficient multibody interactions, including contacts. It is
     used to simulate deformable objects as well, with some degree of control on the elasticity and damping
     coefficients of bodies and contact interactions. The backend is fully in C++. Recently, a new version
     called MuJoCo XLA (MJX) was released for parallel simulation using GPUs (or TPUs).

   • Raisim. This is a CPU-based multi-body physics engine for Robotics and AI. It is specialized in rigid-
     body simulations and can be used through its C++ library. It is full closed-source. More information
     can be found on the website.

   • Brax Freeman et al. (2021). This is a differentiable physics-based engine from Google Research, written
     in JAX for use on acceleration hardware. It is efficient for single-device simulation, and also scalable to
     parallel simulation on multiple devices. The package includes a suite of tasks for benchmarking.

   • Isaac Gym Makoviychuk et al. (2021). This is a widely used simulator for robot learning from NVIDIA.
     When it was released, the novelty was GPU-based simulation for parallelization. It naturally supports
     NVIDIA GPUs.

12.3     Sim2Real
The sim-to-real transfer is a critical challenge in robotics, where policies or models trained in simulation are
deployed in real-world environments. The discrepancies between simulated and real-world conditions, known
as the sim-to-real gap, often lead to a significant drop in performance when transferring from simulation to
reality. This lecture led us to explore the key methodologies used to bridge this gap. In the following, we
present the key Components in sim-to-real transfer.

12.4     Parallel Simulation
Leveraging parallel simulation allows the generation of vast amounts of data . This is crucial for training
complex models, especially in reinforcement learning, where extensive exploration is necessary, and it enables
the use of computational resources effectively to simulate multiple scenarios simultaneously.

12.5     Domain Randomization
Domain randomization is a widely-used technique where various aspects of the simulation are altered during
training. This includes physical properties, environmental conditions, and sensory inputs.The goal is to
expose the model to a wide range of variations so that it learns to generalize beyond the exact parameters of
the simulation. Specifically, when we train the RL policy, we can take the following terms into consideration.

   • Dynamics Randomization. This approach focuses on altering the physical parameters of the
     simulation’s dynamic model for each training rollout. Parameters such as mass, friction coefficients,
     and external force perturbations are varied systematically. For instance, the mass of objects or robotic
     components might be adjusted to account for manufacturing tolerances or payload changes. Friction
     coefficients between contact surfaces are modified to simulate different materials or wear conditions.
     Additionally, random external forces, like wind or minor collisions, are introduced to mimic unexpected
     disturbances. By exposing the model to a wide range of physical scenarios, it learns policies that are not
     overly specialized to a narrow set of dynamics, enhancing its adaptability and robustness in real-world
     applications.

   • Visual Randomization. In this method, visual elements of the simulation are varied extensively.
     Textures of objects and environments are randomized to prevent the model from relying on specific
     visual cues that may not be present in the real world. Lighting conditions are altered, including changes
     in brightness, shadows, and reflections, to simulate different times of day or varying weather conditions.
     Camera parameters such as angles, positions, resolutions, and lens distortions are modified to represent
     different viewpoints and sensor imperfections. Moreover, noise and visual artifacts like motion blur are
     added to simulate real sensor noise. This extensive variability in visual inputs helps the model become
     more robust to changes in visual conditions, ensuring that it can recognize objects and scenes under
     varied and unforeseen circumstances.

12.6     Real World RL Successes
12.7     OpenAI dexterous in-hand manipulation
The manipulation with dexterous hand Andrychowicz et al. (2020) explores sim-to-real transfer, where policies
are trained entirely in simulation and then deployed on real-world robots. Due to the complexity of dexterous
in-hand manipulation, this transfer presents a significant challenge, often referred to as the "reality gap."
The simulation environment used in this research differs substantially from the real-world scenario, and
thus policies trained in simulation often fail when deployed directly on physical robots due to differences in
physical parameters, contact dynamics, and sensor noise.
To address the reality gap, the paper employs domain randomization, a technique that involves randomizing
various aspects of the simulated environment to make the learned policies robust to differences between
simulation and the real world. The key elements of domain randomization in this paper include:

   • Observation Noise: Gaussian noise is added to the simulated observations to mimic the kind of noise
     the sensors might experience in reality. Both correlated (constant over an episode) and uncorrelated
     noise are added to policy observations, such as fingertip positions and object orientation. Additionally,
     simulated marker placement is slightly altered to introduce further uncertainty.

   • Physics Randomizations: Physical parameters in the simulation are randomized to reflect the variability
     and uncertainties of real-world physics, At the start of each episode, key physical parameters such
     as masses, friction coefficients, and joint damping are sampled from distributions centered around
     calibrated values obtained from the real robot. These parameters remain fixed during the episode to
     maintain consistency while still exposing the policy to a range of possible physical conditions.

   • Timing Noise:The timing of environment steps is varied by simulating each environment step as multiple
     physics simulator steps (e.g., 10 MuJoCo steps). This introduces variability in the simulation’s temporal
     dynamics, helping the policy adapt to different execution speeds and timing irregularities that may
     occur in real systems.

   • Unmodeled Effects: Effects that are not directly modeled in the simulation but exist in the real world
     are simulated by randomizing certain aspects. For instance, The physical robot hand uses tendon
     actuation, which introduces backlash—a slackness or lost motion when changing direction—that is not
     present in direct joint actuation models like MuJoCo’s default. To simulate this, a backlash model is
     incorporated. Each joint has two parameters δ−1 and δ+1 , representing the amount of backlash in the
     positive and negative directions, respectively. These parameters are obtained through calibration and

   • Visual Appearance Randomizations: To generalize the vision model to real-world scenarios, aspects such

     deployed on physical robots.

12.8     Case: Outdoor high-speed flight
The paper successfully used a combination of privileged learning, domain randomization (particularly
involving realistic sensor noise and depth representation), lightweight network design, and multimodal
trajectory prediction to enable effective sim-to-real transfer for high-speed quadrotor flightLoquercio et al.
(2021). These techniques helped minimize the reality gap, allowing the quadrotor to perform challenging
navigation tasks without requiring fine-tuning in real environments. Specifically, the author proposed to do
the following things to make it work in the real world.

   • Depth Representation as Input: The authors chose depth images as the primary input representation
     rather than RGB images. Depth images are more robust to variations in visual texture, lighting, and
     color differences that typically cause discrepancies between simulated and real-world environments.
     Since the depth modality is relatively consistent across simulation and reality, this choice helps mitigate
     sim-to-real issues, allowing for better generalization from simulated training environments to real-world
     settings.

   • Lightweight Neural Network Architecture: The network architecture was designed to be lightweight,
     which is essential for enabling real-time inference on the limited computational resources available
     onboard the quadrotor. The low computational demand not only facilitates practical deployment but
     also ensures the latency between perception and action is minimized, which is crucial for maintaining
     performance when transitioning to real-world flight..

   • Multimodal Training and Robustness to Perception Failures: The neural network outputs a set of
     possible trajectories along with associated collision costs. This multimodal approach ensures that
     the network can provide multiple solutions that might be applicable to different scenarios, effectively
     handling the unpredictable nature of real environments. The network was trained using a multihypothesis
     winner-takes-all loss, which helps avoid committing to a single trajectory when multiple viable paths
     exist. This enables the system to adapt to complex real-world environments even in the presence of
     noisy or partial observations.

   • Zero-Shot Generalization: After training in simulation, the policy was directly deployed in real-world
     environments without any fine-tuning, which is termed "zero-shot" transfer. The environments tested
     were diverse and included both natural (e.g., forests, snow-covered terrains) and human-made (e.g.,
     collapsed buildings) environments, showcasing the generalization capability achieved by the domain
     randomization and the depth-based input representation. The experiments demonstrated that the
     policy could navigate complex and unseen environments successfully at high speeds, emphasizing the
     robustness of the sim-to-real transfer strategy.
