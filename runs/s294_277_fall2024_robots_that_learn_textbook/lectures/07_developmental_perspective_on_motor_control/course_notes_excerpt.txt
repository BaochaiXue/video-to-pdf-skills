8.6     Development of Embodied Cognition: Six Lessons from Babies
Based on the work of Linda Smith and Michael Gasser, six key lessons for embodied cognition are:

    1. Be multi-modal: Intelligence develops through the integration of multiple sensory modalities (e.g.,
       vision, hearing, touch). Babies learn by correlating time-locked sensory inputs, allowing each modality
       to educate the others without explicit external teaching.

    2. Be incremental: Development occurs step-by-step, with infants gradually gaining new capabili-
       ties. This incremental approach enables them to build on earlier experiences, with the sequence of
       developmental milestones playing a critical role in shaping intelligence.

    3. Be physical: Interaction with the physical world provides a foundation for higher cognitive functions.
       Infants use their bodies and spatial contexts to simplify complex cognitive tasks, effectively offloading
       some mental processes onto the environment.

  4. Explore: Spontaneous, non-goal-directed exploration helps babies discover tasks and solutions in-
     dependently. Through movement and play, they experiment, learn, and develop problem-solving
     skills.

  5. Be social: Social interactions with caregivers scaffold learning. Babies engage in mutual, responsive
     exchanges, where mature partners provide structure and guide development. These interactions are
     multimodal and help bind language and cognitive skills.

  6. Use language: Language introduces symbolic reasoning and enables the development of abstract
     thinking. It acts as a shared system for categorizing the world and facilitates the creation of higher-order
     generalizations, enhancing cognitive and communicative capabilities.

8.10    General In-Hand Object Rotation with Vision and Touch
8.11    Overview
RotateIt is a system that enables dexterous in-hand object rotation along multiple axes using a combination
of vision, touch, and proprioceptive sensing. The system leverages:

   • Multimodal sensory inputs to infer object shapes and physical properties.

   • A visuotactile transformer for robust policy training in simulation, with successful sim-to-real transfer.

This method significantly improves upon prior approaches by generalizing to diverse objects without fine-
tuning.

8.12    Oracle Policy Training
The training process begins with an oracle policy, which has access to privileged information such as:

   • Object shape encoded using a point cloud processed by a PointNet encoder.

   • Physical properties like mass, friction, and center of mass.

The oracle policy uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and a carefully crafted reward function to
optimize rotation performance across three principal axes (x, y, and z).

8.13    Visuotactile Policy Training
In real-world deployment, privileged information is inaccessible. Instead, RotateIt uses:

   • Vision-based depth sensing to capture object geometry.

   • Tactile feedback from omnidirectional touch sensors for contact information.


                                                      49
   • A visuotactile transformer to integrate multimodal inputs (vision, touch, proprioception, and action
     history).

This transformer minimizes the gap between simulation and real-world scenarios, achieving robust in-hand
manipulation policies.

8.14    Results and Analysis
Quantitative Improvements: RotateIt outperforms baseline models by incorporating vision and touch,
achieving higher rotation rewards and stability. The addition of object shape encoding further enhances
generalization.

Real-World Application: In real-world tests, RotateIt successfully rotates objects over multiple axes with
diverse geometries, demonstrating robust sim-to-real transfer. Objects include cubes, spheres, and irregular
shapes.

8.15    Conclusion and Implications
RotateIt highlights the critical role of multimodal sensory integration in robot dexterity. By combining vision
and touch with advanced learning techniques, the system represents a significant step toward general-purpose
in-hand manipulation.

8.16    Learning Visual Locomotion with Cross-Modal Supervision
8.17    Overview
This section introduces Cross-Modal Supervision (CMS), a novel technique for training visual walking
policies. CMS enables quadruped robots to navigate complex terrains using a monocular RGB camera and
proprioception. The approach allows continuous learning in real-world conditions, improving performance
with minimal data.

8.18    Core Insights and Methodology
The CMS framework operates under the principle of using time-shifted proprioception to supervise visual
modules. Key components include:

   • Blind Policy πblind : Trained in simulation, this policy lacks visual input but uses proprioception for
     terrain estimation. This policy can be trained in simulation, and transferred to the real world. This
     policy does not have lookahead and uses proprioception to estimate the terrain under the feet.

   • Lookahead Policy π: Combines visual predictions with proprioception to anticipate and adapt to
     upcoming terrain changes. This policy can also be trained in simulation. It predicts the terrain at 15cm
     lookahead in front of the robot.

   • Vision Module: Trained in the real world using CMS, the module predicts terrain geometry from
     egocentric RGB input.

CMS leverages real-world data collected during deployment, continually refining the visual model through
self-supervised learning.

where qt ∈ R12 is current joint position, q′t ∈ R12 is Joint velocity, and at−1 ∈ R12 is previous action. The

                                                  zt = µ(et )                                             (24)
where µ is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), and et represents environmental inputs. The terrain geometry γt
is derived as:

                                                    γt = δ(ht )
                                                                                                             (25)
                                                γt+∆t = δ(ht+∆t )

where δ is an MLP, and ht represents terrain features. Note that γt+∆t is the predicted terrain 15cm in front

                                            at = πblind (xt , zt , γt )

                                            at = π(xt , zt , γt , γt+∆t )

Note that the lookahead policy generates actions based on current terrain and predicted terrain.

Environmental Design They train agents in fractal terrains and parameterized inclines and stairs to elicit
robust locomotion. They build stairs that are comfortable for humans to mimic the real-world environment
as closely as possible.

Simulation to Real-World Transfer We summarize how CMS transfers its policy trained in a simulator
to the real world.

   • Training begins with a blind policy (πblind ) in simulation on diverse terrains, including fractal patterns
     and parameterized staircases.

   • The vision module is trained to predict future proprioceptive terrain estimates by minimizing the CMS
     error.

   • The lookahead policy (π) combines real-world visual data with simulation-learned policies, achieving
     robust sim-to-real transfer.

8.19     Experimental Results
Lifelong Learning: CMS enables robots to adapt to new environments with minimal real-world data. For
instance, performance on stairs improved from 40% to 100% success with only 30 minutes of training data
collected over four days.

Generalization: The system demonstrated robust navigation across diverse terrains, including unseen
staircases, steep inclines, and discrete surfaces.

Visual Plasticity: Using the "prism adaptation test," the system adapted to shifted visual inputs in under
a minute, showcasing the flexibility of the visual policy.

8.20     Conclusion
The CMS framework exemplifies the power of cross-modal supervision in robot learning. By leveraging
proprioceptive data to train visual models, the approach achieves significant performance improvements in
visual locomotion with minimal real-world data. This method lays the foundation for lifelong learning and
adaptability in autonomous robotic systems.

8.21     Learning to Poke by Poking: Experiential Learning of Intuitive Physics
8.22     Overview
This section discusses an experiential learning paradigm that enables robots to develop intuitive physics
models. By randomly interacting with objects through poking, robots can learn to predict object dynamics
and plan actions. The approach combines forward and inverse dynamics models to achieve generalization and
decision-making capabilities.

8.23     Core Methodology
8.23.1   Data Collection
   • A Baxter robot was trained through over 100,000 pokes, collecting more than 400 hours of interaction
     data with 16 distinct objects.

   • Each interaction recorded the state of objects before and after a poke using RGB images.

   • Actions were represented as pokes with randomized location, direction, and length.




                                                      52
8.23.2    Joint Modeling of Forward and Inverse Dynamics
   • Forward Model: Predicts the next state of the object given the current state and action.

   • Inverse Model: Infers the action required to transition between two states.

   • These models are jointly trained to leverage complementary strengths:

         – The inverse model helps learn a feature space relevant for abstract event prediction.
         – The forward model regularizes the feature space, avoiding degenerate solutions.

8.24     Evaluation and Results
8.24.1    Generalization Capabilities
The robot demonstrated the ability to:

   • Successfully displace both familiar and unseen objects to desired goal locations.

   • Operate in environments with distractor objects, maintaining accuracy.

8.24.2    Performance Analysis
   • The joint model outperformed both inverse-only models and baseline approaches, such as a blob model,
     particularly in tasks requiring large object displacements.

   • Simulation studies confirmed that forward models effectively regularize inverse models, improving
     generalization with smaller datasets.

8.24.3    Limitations
The system struggled with tasks requiring navigation around obstacles due to greedy planning strategies,
leading to suboptimal trajectories.

8.25     Conclusion and Future Work
This study establishes the potential of experiential learning for developing intuitive physics in robots. Future
directions include:

   • Exploring continuous control for more precise manipulation.

   • Extending the framework to deformable objects and complex environments.

   • Integrating forward models into planning strategies for improved efficiency.

8.26     Humanoid Locomotion as Next Token Prediction
8.27     Overview
This section introduces a novel framework for real-world humanoid locomotion modeled as a next token
prediction problem. By leveraging autoregressive generative modeling, the method predicts sensorimotor
trajectories, enabling humanoids to walk in diverse and complex real-world environments.




                                                      53
8.28     Core Methodology
8.28.1   Next Token Prediction Framework
   • Humanoid locomotion is reformulated as a sequence modeling task. Sensorimotor trajectories are
     tokenized, where each token corresponds to a sensory input or motor command.

   • A causal transformer model predicts the next token in the sequence, given prior tokens, encompassing
     both sensory and motor modalities.

   • Missing modalities (e.g., actions in human motion capture or video data) are addressed using masked
     tokens, allowing for joint training on heterogeneous data.

8.28.2   Training Data Sources
The model was trained on a diverse dataset of sensorimotor trajectories from:
  1. Neural network policies trained in simulation.

  2. Model-based controllers from a humanoid simulator.

  3. Human motion capture datasets retargeted to humanoid morphology.

  4. Internet videos of human motion processed via inverse kinematics.

8.28.3   Model Architecture
   • The transformer architecture utilizes modality-specific tokenization, temporal embeddings, and autore-
     gressive attention mechanisms.

   • Positional embeddings encode temporal sequence information, enabling predictions aligned with sensory
     or motor modalities.

   • During training, a mean squared error loss is used to minimize the difference between predicted and
     ground truth tokens.

8.29     Experimental Results
8.29.1   Real-World Deployment
   • A full-sized Digit humanoid robot successfully walked across various terrains in San Francisco, including
     concrete, asphalt, dirt, and sand.

   • The model demonstrated generalization to novel environments and robust adherence to velocity
     commands.

8.29.2   Comparative Performance
   • Quantitative evaluations showed lower trajectory tracking error compared to reinforcement learning-
     based policies, especially for complex commands.

   • The model’s prediction error correlated strongly with its real-world tracking accuracy, highlighting the
     importance of generative modeling.

8.29.3   Unseen Command Generalization
The transformer model extrapolated to commands not present during training, such as walking backward,
demonstrating its adaptability and versatility.

This study demonstrates the feasibility of using autoregressive generative modeling for humanoid locomotion.
By leveraging diverse data sources and addressing missing modalities, the approach offers a scalable and
