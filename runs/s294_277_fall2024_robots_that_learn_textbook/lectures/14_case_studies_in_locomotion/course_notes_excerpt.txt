                             Lecture 9A: Locomotion for Quadrupeds
                                                      Abstract
          This lecture covered quadruped locomotion, a challenging area in robotics that involves enabling
       four-legged robots to walk, adapt, and navigate in various environments. Inspired by biological principles
       and recent advances in RL, quadruped locomotion leverages simulations, pattern generators, and adaptive
       controllers to achieve robust and efficient movement. The following sections outline key approaches to
       quadruped locomotion and highlight recent advances that allow for natural, adaptive gaits and navigation
       using sensor inputs.


16.1      Introduction




                             Figure 64: Prominent Examples of Quadruped Robots

Quadruped locomotion in robotics requires precise control, adaptability to different terrains, and effective
navigation in dynamic environments. Leveraging insights from biology and recent advances in reinforcement
learning (RL), researchers have developed techniques that allow four-legged robots such as Boston Dynamics’
Spot and MIT’s Mini Cheetah (Figure 64) to learn versatile, adaptive gaits. From simulated training to
real-world adaptation with sensor data, these robots gain stability and agility through learned, natural
movement patterns. This section explores the advancements in quadruped locomotion, including adaptive
gaits, Rapid Motor Adaptation (RMA), and vision-based navigation.

16.2      Pattern Generators for Gait Control
Early quadruped locomotion systems used preprogrammed Pattern Generators for basic gait control. These
generators provided fixed, repetitive gait patterns, mimicking simple biological circuits for energy-efficient
locomotion:

   • Fixed Gait Generators (1980s): Hildebrand (1965) analyzed different gain patterns on horses and came
     up with numerical expressions for them. The interest for biological motor control increased in the 1980s.
     The first successful legged walking machine was produced from from CMU. In Raibert et al. (1989),
     these preprogrammed circuits control repeated motion patterns and enable efficient movement over
     predictable surfaces. However, they lack adaptability to changes in the terrain. It is also important to
     realize that the limited compute power dictated these research progress back then.

16.3    Reinforcement Learning for Natural Gait Adaptation
Modern approaches seek to learn gaits “naturally” using RL, allowing robots to adaptively choose efficient
gaits based on environmental feedback. This approach aims to replicate how babies learn to walk through
trial and error. To avoid the high cost and time required for physical trials, this RL process is conducted in
simulation, where training can run faster than real time and in parallel, making it significantly more efficient
and cost-effective.

   • Reward Mechanisms: The robot’s reward is based on minimizing energy use and ground impact
     forces, encouraging efficient and stable movements. Fu et al. (2021) found that doing this, along with
     tracking different target speeds, resulted in the emergence of different gaits that we observe in nature in
     four-legged animals, such as walking, trotting and galloping (as seen in Figure 65).

   • Single Policy Across Terrains: By learning a generalized policy, the robot can adapt its gait across a
     range of terrains without requiring specific gait generators for each one. The robot receives ground truth
     hints about the environment, such as the friction and terrain height in Figure 66. Note the difference
     from older approaches discussed in the previous section, where different controllers were used depending
     on the environment.


16.4    Simulation-to-Real Transfer and Rapid Motor Adaptation
Transitioning from simulation to the real world presents unique challenges as the robot no longer has access
to the simulated environment’s “ground truth.” Kumar et al. (2021) introduce an adaptation module that
helps to bridge this gap by estimating environmental factors in real time. In this process of policy training,
three models are trained for effective adaptation:

   • Base Policy (RL): Governs basic locomotion learned in simulation.

   • Environmental Factor Encoder (Supervised): Encodes features of the current terrain.

16.5    Speed-Adaptive Gaits for Efficient Locomotion
Quadruped robots can achieve different gaits, such as walking, trotting, or running, by training policies that
target specific speeds. Speed variation is achieved through adjustments in the reward structure:

   • Variable Reward Terms: By changing reward terms to prioritize different speeds, the robot can learn
     distinct movement patterns for various velocities.

   • Policy Distillation: Policies for different speeds can be distilled into a single general policy, which allows
     the robot to seamlessly transition between gaits based on its desired speed and energy efficiency.

16.6    Navigation Using Vision and Proprioception
So far, the discussion of quadruped locomotion has missed a vital component, perception, which allows
robots to handle complex terrains more effectively. Although animals-and even robots-can navigate diverse


                                                       96
environments using proprioception alone, incorporating vision significantly enhances navigation capabilities.
Agarwal et al. (2023) present an end-to-end system that enables a quadruped robot to traverse stairs, curbs,
and varied terrain using a single, front-facing depth camera, with learned gait patterns tailored to the robot’s
size and setup. This system leverages egocentric vision to maintain a memory of the terrain under the robot’s
hind feet, demonstrating robust performance in both simulated and real-world environments.
In a complementary approach, Fu et al. (2022) developed VP-Nav, a navigation system that combines vision
and proprioception to enhance safety and adaptability. Using onboard cameras for occupancy mapping
and proprioceptive feedback to detect unexpected obstacles (e.g., glass walls, slippery surfaces), VP-Nav
generates dynamic path plans suited to the terrain. The system’s layered control, integrating high-level
path planning with low-level locomotion, outperforms wheeled robots and demonstrates strong real-world
navigation capabilities on a quadruped platform.

   • Hierarchical Controller: This setup includes high-level planning based on target locations and low-level
     control for adjusting gait and speed.

   • Vision-Based Mapping: While maps as seen in Figure 68 are commonly used for navigation, real-world
     conditions (e.g., camera shake) often limit their reliability.

   • Learning Environmental Parameters: Instead of traditional mapping, robots learn environmental and
     geometric properties through sensor fusion of visual and proprioceptive data. This enables the robot to
     adapt its movements based on real-time observations, facilitating robust and adaptable navigation.

                             Lecture 9B: Locomotion for humanoids
                                                    Abstract
           In this lecture, we will explore locomotion but more tailored towards bipeds like humanoids. Most
       of the lecture emphasizes on how locomotion is more difficult for biped robots like humanoids than
       quadruped robots.


17.1      Introduction
Locomotion in the context of bipeds presents significantly greater challenges than for quadrupeds. This is
because quadrupeds have 4 legs which makes it easier to balance than with 2 legs. This is challenging also
because with 2 legs the system is in an unstable equilibrium as compared to with 4 legs that can provide
contact force in 4 directions to balance the torque in all directions.

17.2      Some initial works
Some initial works on humanoid locomotion are dated back as early as 1990s. One of the most notable robot
that came up was that honda asimo Chestnutt et al. (2005) which could perform hard-coded stable gait for
biped walking. These robots notably used zero-moment point (ZMP) Miao et al. (2023) which aims at trying
to maintain zero torque at the axis passing through COM. For walking, they have one leg always on the
ground and move the hip to correct for some rotation. Some of the earlier boston dynamics robots ATLAS
Feng et al. (2015) also notably uses model predictive control (MPC) assuming known dynamic model and
has been able to perform highly dynamic tasks such as jumping over obstacles, parkour, back flip etc. It is
important to note that these approaches are really good only in structured environment. This is because for
structured environments, it is easier to forward simulate how robot will react given a sequence of control
commands and thus use it to develop a MPC controller. However, it is difficult to make these approaches
work in general environments without assuming any structure of the environment. For robust operation of
humanoids on unstructured general environments, we need to use learning based techniques which we shall
discuss in the next section

17.3      Learning based techniques
Recent work by Radosavovic et al. (2023) has demonstrated significant progress in developing end-to-end
neural net controller trained with reinforcement learning that can walk slowly in wide range of terrains. The
underlying structure of the controller is that it takes a history of joint angles and velocities as proprioception,
and uses a causal transformer to predict the action in terms of the residual on joint angles. This end-to-end
transformer is trained with reinforcement learning in simulation with a wide range of settings in terms of
differences in terrain, friction etc. as domain adaptation. The reason behind domain adaptation in simulation
is that the controller becomes robust to all of these changes in the terrain and that the real world could be
treated as yet another simulation within the training distribution.
One interesting skill that emerges with diverse training is the arm swings that were not explicitly instructed in
the reward design. The reward design is only based on energy conservation. This phenomenon is well studied
in literature Alghooneh and Wu (2014) and has been shown that swinging hands is the most energy-efficient
technique to walk for humans
Another interesting behavior observed is adaptation of walking style based on surface. During experimentation,
the robot walked with more frequent steps with less height when on a slope. This behavior is not pre-
programmed but is entirely emergent. This could be possible only because of in-context adaptation enabled by

17.4    Humanoid Locomotion as Next Token Prediction
The recent work Radosavovic et al. (2024) attempts into using the human walking videos to train a transformer
in a fashion similar to the next token prediction unsupervised learning scheme prevalent in language modeling.
This work includes 3 phases. The first phase is using internet videos of humans walking as well as motion
capture pieces. The humans have a different morphology that the humanoid, hence the motion needs to
be re-targeted to cater to a humanoid. The key takeaway with this decision of using noisy and re-targeted
motion from internet videos is that we only need to mimic the style of walking from the data so it is still fine
that the data is noisy as we prefer large noisy data over small perfect clean data. This data is then used
in an unsupervised fashion to train transformer (same structure as described in previous section) with task
to recreate some missing frames that are intentionally dropped in the sequence. One interesting conclusion
from this work is that the Gait learned from mimicking humans is very similar to the one trained with RL
and perhaps more smooth and better. One more interesting emergent behavior observed was that if the
model is trained only on data with forward walking, it was still able to recreate pieces of backward walking if
prompted that way. In conclusion, this approach turns out to be far more sample efficient than reinforcement
learning. However, an obvious drawback is that it can only learn skills present in the data.

17.5    Combining the benefits

et al. (2023). This work combines the benefits of both reinforcement learning and human mimicking through
next token prediction. This work includes 2 steps. The first step involves using human mimicking to initialize
the policy which is later fine-tuned with RL in simulation on more challenging terrain as the second step.
The intuition behind this is that a robot that can walk on a flat terrain is far more capable of walking on a
challenging terrain than a robot that is not able to walk as all. This can be witnessed from Figure 72 where
