2.1    Walking
2.2    Gait Cycle
The gait cycle of walking consists of two main phases: the stance phase and the swing phase. The stance
phase includes foot contact, foot flat, heel off, and toe off. The swing phase involves peak knee flexion and
ends with foot contact. A key characteristic of walking is that at least one foot is always on the ground,
which distinguishes it from running, where both feet can be in the air. This is also the definition of running
in the Olympics.

2.3    Basic Concepts
A stride is defined as the distance between two consecutive foot contacts of the same foot. The normal stride
length for an adult is approximately 1.2-1.4 meters, although this can vary depending on the individual. The
average walking speed is about 4.5 km/h (3 mph).

2.5    Kinematics vs Dynamics
In the study of walking, kinematics focuses on the geometry of joint angles, while dynamics deals with the
forces involved. External forces can be measured using force plates, which provide valuable data for analyzing
gait.

2.6    Center of Mass and Energy
During walking, the center of mass of the body oscillates vertically. This oscillation leads to an interesting
interplay between gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy. These two forms of energy oscillate out
of phase with each other, similar to the behavior of a pendulum system.

2.7    Pendulum Models
Walking can be modeled using two types of pendulums. The stance limb is represented as an inverted
pendulum, while the swing limb is modeled as a double pendulum. These models help in understanding the
mechanics of human gait.

The Froude number is particularly important for comparing gaits across different sizes and gravitational
environments. When walking on the moon, the reduced gravitational force (approximately 1/6 of Earth’s
gravity) significantly affects your movement. As the gravitational acceleration g is much smaller on the moon,
each step becomes more like a leap. On the moon, humans become like kangaroos (Figure 10).

2.9    Passive Dynamic Walker
A passive dynamic walker is a mechanical device that demonstrates the efficiency of human walking. It can
walk continuously on a slight downward slope without any energy input other than gravity. This illustrates
the potential for creating energy-efficient bipedal robots.

2.10    Energy Consumption
Energy consumption during walking can be measured using oxygen masks. The cost of transport, which is
the energy required to move a unit mass over a unit distance, varies with walking speed. Studies have shown

that there is an optimum walking speed of around 1.3-1.4 m/s where energy consumption is minimized.

2.11    Human Anatomy and Joint Angles
Understanding human anatomy is crucial for analyzing gait. Key terms include abduction (moving away
from the body), adduction (moving towards the body), flexion (closing a joint), and extension (opening a
joint). The angles of various joints, including the hip, knee, and ankle, change throughout the gait cycle in
characteristic patterns.

2.12    Running
2.13    Gait Cycle Differences
Running differs from walking primarily in the inclusion of a flight phase, where both feet are off the ground.
The double stance phase present in walking is eliminated in running.

2.14    Forces and Energy
Running produces significantly higher forces than walking, with peak forces reaching up to twice the body
weight. Unlike in walking, the gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy are in phase during running.

2.15    Spring-Mass Model
Running can be modeled as an inverted pendulum with a spring. This spring-mass model represents the leg’s
ability to store and release energy during each stride, contributing to the efficiency of running.

2.16    Comparison to Walking
Running is more energetically expensive than walking and produces greater forces. One advantage robot have
over humans is the ability to store energy in springs (both physical and electronic—consider regenerative
braking).

2.17    Animal Gaits
While humans primarily use two gaits (walking and running), quadrupeds have a wider range of gaits. These
include walking, ambling, pacing, trotting, cantering, and galloping or running. The cost of transport varies
with speed for different gaits, with each gait being most efficient within a certain speed range. This has
emergeed in reinforcement learning policies: by rewarding different speed requirements, researchers can
observe how different gaits emerge for different speeds Fu et al. (2021).
